Why this exists

The research paper tells you what they built.
It never tells you what broke.

Seniors graduate. Their code goes private. Their contact info disappears. What remains is a formal paper — the polished result, none of the journey. Every year, the next batch starts from zero and pays the same tuition in mistakes. This archive is what should have existed all along.

Browse the archive Read the full story
01

The problem

A research paper is written for a committee. It answers a specific set of questions in a specific order — methodology, implementation, results, conclusion — and it answers them in the voice of someone who already knows how the story ends. By the time it's printed, every dead end has been quietly deleted. Every wrong turn has been reframed as a considered decision. Every three-week detour that went nowhere doesn't appear at all.

That's not dishonesty. That's what a paper is for. But it means the paper cannot answer the questions a junior actually has. It cannot tell you which library looked perfect on paper and collapsed under real data. It cannot tell you which part of the project took four times longer than expected, or which tool you should install on day one so you don't lose a week in month three.

The paper records what survived. It has no record of what was abandoned — and that's the part that costs the next person the most.

The people who could answer those questions have already graduated. Their email doesn't work. Their GitHub is private or gone. If you wanted to ask them anything, there's no door left to knock on. The journey they took — the real one, the messy one — left no trail behind it. Only the polished paper, written for someone who will never build anything from it.

02

What that actually costs

The loss isn't abstract. It shows up as specific, avoidable pain for the next person who tries to build something similar.

01

Repeat the same mistakes

A senior spent three weeks trying a framework that didn't scale. The paper doesn't mention it. A junior picks the same framework, spends the same three weeks, and arrives at the same conclusion. Nothing was learned, because nothing was written down.

02

Rebuild from scratch

The senior's GitHub repo is gone. The dataset they spent a month assembling no longer exists. The junior has to start from zero — not because the work wasn't done, but because nothing about it was preserved in a form anyone could pick up.

03

Lose the mentor

The senior spent a year learning things no book covers — how to choose a supervisor, how to structure the write-up, how to keep a project alive when motivation drops. That knowledge walks out the door at graduation. Nobody downstream ever gets to ask.

04

The paper can't be questioned

A PDF is one-directional. You read it. You can't ask it why a particular model was chosen over another, or whether the dataset was actually clean, or how long the deployment took. Every question you'd want to ask dies in your head, because there's no one to ask.

03

Where we looked — and what we found

Before building this, we tried to find the information that already existed. We assumed someone, somewhere, had documented the previous batches' work. We were wrong. What we found instead:

The research paper exists — but that's it

Most seniors submit a research paper to the department. It describes the final solution in academic language, but says nothing about what went wrong along the way, which libraries turned out to be dead ends, or how long anything actually took.

No way to reach them

Even when we knew a project was in a field we wanted to pursue, there was no contact information — no email, no phone, no handle. There was no way to ask "how did you approach the dataset?" or "would you do it the same way again?"

No code, no links, no trail

The GitHub repository, if there was one, was either private or never shared. The live demo, if it existed, had been taken down. What remained was a title on a notice board and a vague memory of a demo.

And then they graduated

The institution keeps the paperwork. The student keeps the experience. Between the two, nothing connects them. Every year, the same knowledge gets rebuilt from scratch by people who had no way to know it already existed.

04

What we built instead

Project Vault is a single place where a senior can document the parts of their project that never make it into the paper — and where a junior can find the person behind the project and actually reach them. Every entry captures the same set of questions, so you can compare across projects instead of reading ten unrelated write-ups.

For each project we ask seven things:

  • What was the real problem? Not the sanitised academic version — the actual pain point the team set out to solve.
  • What did they aim to build, and what were the measurable objectives? So the next person knows what "success" looked like for them.
  • How did they actually build it? The approach, the phases, the trade-offs, the technologies chosen and why.
  • What went wrong along the way? The development challenges, the bugs that broke them, the parts that took three times longer than expected.
  • What mistakes did they make? The most valuable section. If a junior reads nothing else, this should save them weeks.
  • What did they learn? The technical skills, the mindset shifts, and the honest reflection on what they'd do differently.
  • How can someone reach them? GitHub, live demo, email, social — so the conversation can continue past the graduation ceremony.
05

The part that changes everything

A year of building teaches lessons no textbook covers. The senior who spent twelve months on a project knows exactly which decisions mattered, which ones were wasted effort, and how they'd approach it if they started again. That knowledge is the most valuable thing a junior could inherit — far more useful than the paper, because it answers the questions the paper can't.

If that senior is reachable, they can guide. If a junior is building the same kind of project in the same field, a thirty-minute conversation with the person who did it last year is worth more than a month of trial and error. It's not a formal mentorship — it's one conversation, one reply, one "don't waste time on X, use Y instead." And it can save weeks.

The most useful thing in this archive isn't the code. It's the person who wrote it — and whether you can reach them.

That's why every project entry includes contact details. Not because every junior will reach out, but because the door should exist. A senior who documents their project and leaves a GitHub link is offering to be a mentor for the next person. A junior who finds that link has someone to ask — not a paper, not a demo, a person who has been where they are about to go.

06

Why this matters

For juniors

You don't have to start from nothing. You can see what worked before you, what didn't, and where the unexplored opportunities are. And when you hit a wall, you can reach the person who hit it last year.

For seniors

A year of work gets a permanent home. Not buried in a department filing cabinet, not lost when the GitHub repo goes private — a living record people can find, read, and ask you about for years.

For the department

A growing, self-maintaining archive of what students have built — useful for open days, for accreditation, and for seeing which fields the college is actually strong in.

For the next batch, and the one after that

Every entry makes the next one easier to write. Over four or five years, the archive becomes institutional memory — the kind that normally evaporates with every graduation.

07

Who this is for

If you are a Final year student

Choosing a project this semester. Use the archive to pick a field, see what's been tried, and reach the senior whose project is closest to what you want to build.

If you are a Recent graduate

Your year taught you things worth passing on. Add your project — it takes about an hour, and it will help juniors for years. Even a link to your GitHub is more than they have now.

If you are a Faculty member

Looking for a lightweight way to keep track of what students are building — and to help juniors find mentors from previous batches. The whole archive is open and readable.

If you are Anyone curious

Wondering what final year students in your field are actually capable of building — and what they learned doing it. Browse, filter, read.

✎

An honest note

This archive is only as useful as what people put into it. If a senior writes a sanitised version of their project — the same polished summary that goes in the paper — it adds nothing. The value is in the messy parts: what broke, what was harder than expected, what took three attempts to get right. And in leaving a door open: a link, an email, a handle someone can actually use.

So if you contribute, don't write the version you'd submit to a committee. Write the version you'd tell a friend who's about to start the same thing — and then tell them how to find you.
